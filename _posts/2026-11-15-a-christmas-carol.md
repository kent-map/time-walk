We all know the plot of Charles Dickens’ _A Christmas Carol_. Miserly Ebeneezer Scrooge is haunted 
by the ghost of his old business partner Jacob Marley and the three spirits of Christmas. The 
visions they reveal are so disturbing that he renounces his wicked past and, among other acts of 
generosity, lavishes a giant turkey on his overworked and underpaid clerk Bob Cratchit and 
becomes a second father to the man’s disabled son Tiny Tim. 
<br><br>
But few will be aware that the first glimmer of this Yuletide morality tale came to the 
author during a family holiday he spent in Broadstairs in the summer of 1843. 

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:A Christmas Carol-Rackham-093.jpg" %}

Writing from there to a friend on September 1, he revealed he’d had a strange dream whilst 
sleeping in his holiday home, now part of the Royal Albion Hotel. He confided: ‘I dreamed that 
somebody was dead . . . and I was greatly overcome when the news was broken to me (very 
delicately) by a gentlemen in a cocked hat, top boots, and a sheet. Nothing else.’ “Good God,” I 
said. “Is he dead?” “He is as dead, Sir,’ rejoined the gentleman, “as a door-nail.”  
<br><br>
Two weeks later, and still troubled by the dream, Dickens travelled from Broadstairs to 
visit a London charity school struggling to care for dozens of filthy, illiterate street children. A 
shocked Dickens later said he found there ‘a sickening atmosphere, in the midst of taint, dirt and 
pestilence, with all the deadly sins let loose, howling and shrieking at the doors.’

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:Albion Hotel Broadstairs.jpg" %}

On his return to the Thanet resort, he decided to write a ‘sledgehammer’ of an essay to 
highlight the plight of these abandoned boys and girls. But he quickly concluded a book would 
have a bigger impact. And if it could be on sale in time for Christmas – the season of peace on 
earth and good will to all men – then its effect would be so much greater. 
<br><br>
Back at home in London in early October, Dickens at once started on the story that would 
highlight the obscene inequality between the richest and poorest in society. His opening line: 
‘Marley was dead, to begin with’ prompted his thoughts to fly back to Broadstairs and he 
concluded the first paragraph with the words that the strangely dressed gentleman had addressed 
to him in the dream: ‘Old Marley was as dead as a door-nail.’  

{% include embed/image.html src="wc:'Scrooge and the Ghost of Marley' by Arthur Rackham.jpg" %}

Dickens finished the tale in just six weeks. It was delivered to booksellers on December 19 
and its first run had sold out by Christmas Eve. Dickens was ecstatic and threw himself into his 
own festive celebrations. In a letter written on January 2, 1844, he described his Christmas and 
New Year as: ‘Such dinings, such dancings, such blindman’s-buffings, such theatre-goings, such 
kissings-out of old years and kissings-in of new ones . . .’ 
<br><br>
It is over 175 years since Dickens wrote _A Christmas Carol_ but its positive message of 
redemption, with Scrooge renouncing greed and selfishness and embracing compassion and 
generosity, still resonates today. And just think – this timeless story, which has become a literary 
classic, first came blinking into the light . . . in Broadstairs. 
<br><br>
I will leave it to Charles Dickens, through the warming words of his characters Bob Cratchit 
and Tiny Tim, to send seasonal greetings to members of the Broadstairs Dickens Fellowship, their 
families and friends.  
<br><br>
Bob: ‘Merry Christmas to us all, my dears. God bless us!’ Tiny Tim: ‘God bless everyone!’
